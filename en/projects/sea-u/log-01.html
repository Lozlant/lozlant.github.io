Entry 01　2025.09

Theme Research

Starting from 5 sources: how coastal cities are responding to sea-level rise, and what that means for game design.

Research Question

Sea level rise has become a difficult trend to reverse, and many coastal nations and cities are facing challenges brought by this issue. I want to study how coastal cities will respond to the changes caused by rising seas, and what policy choices they will make.

Source Notes

For each source, I noted three things: what I learned, what transformation it suggests for players, and what it means for the game design.

1. Policy Options for Sea Level Rise

McGuire, C. J. (2017). Policy Options. Adapting to Sea Level Rise in the Coastal Zone: Law and Policy Considerations (1st ed., pp. 73–151). Routledge.

  • What I learned: predictions of sea level rise are highly uncertain, so policies need to remain flexible, with space for adjustment and backup plans; in other words, policies may change over time. The chapter introduces the sustainable development framework of Economy within Society within Environment, which made me realize that these three are not separate: a policy that only solves problems in one area may cause chain reactions in others. When evaluating a policy's net benefits, environmental values are often underestimated or ignored; different assumptions in the analysis can lead to completely different results; and the time scale matters, since a short-term economic gain may look positive, but in the long term the net benefit may shift.
  • Transformation: players learn how to analyze policies for sea level rise, assess risks, and make decisions that benefit them in the long term, and gain enough knowledge to take part in discussions and even political action on this issue. The obstacle: balancing policies requires players to have sufficient information and background knowledge.
  • Design implication: build a decision-making system where each policy creates triple impacts on the economy, society and the environment, and players need to balance them. This is the main reference for my policy research.

2. The Floating City Concept

Januszkiewicz, K., Gołębiewski, J., Czarnecki, B., & Turecki, A. (2024). Redefining Urbanism in Perspective of Climate Change: Floating Cities Concept. Arts (Basel), 13(6), 183. doi.org/10.3390/arts13060183

  • What I learned: the article redefines floating cities in the context of sea level rise, reviews modern visions of ocean-based cities such as megastructures and Metabolism, and highlights the idea of building self-sustaining green cities to face future climate change. It made me realize that floating cities could be one way to respond to rising seas, and made me think about what a floating city would need and the challenges behind it, such as construction costs, industrial capitalism, and the gap between technological utopias and social reality. Population, costs, urban cycles and economic structures all play an important role.
  • Transformation: players may become interested in floating city construction and urbanism, and gain the ability to plan urban structures.
  • Design implication: let players explore future policy choices and city-building in the game, and experience how sea level rise might lead to new urban forms and social systems.

3. Public Awareness and Participation

Green, A. (2024). Discussion with Forum Participants. Three Reconstructions of ‘Effectiveness’: Some Implications for State Continuity and Sea-level Rise. Oxford Journal of Legal Studies, 44(2), 201–230. doi.org/10.1093/ojls/gqae003

  • What I learned: in facing the crisis of sea level rise, raising public awareness and making people realize it is a common interest is an important step. This requires policymakers to provide the necessary information so people can understand the benefits and drawbacks of each plan. Policies and technology alone cannot solve the problem; public acceptance and participation are also essential. The conference also pointed out that engineers will play an important role in the crisis, but they need to work together with sociologists, and communication and mutual understanding should be an important part of future engineering education.
  • Transformation: players realize how technology is accepted by society, and are able to show the value of technology.
  • Design implication: the player is not a dictator who can decide everything. They need to put in effort to gain public acceptance of policies.

4. A Narrative Game About Sea Level Rise

Keys, P., & Keys, M. (2022). Creating a climate changed future with the sea level rise interactive-fiction game “Lagos2199.” Ecology and Society, 27(3), art40. doi.org/10.5751/ES-13393-270340

  • What I learned: the article describes the development of a narrative game about sea level rise and argues for the value of narrative games in climate change education: the authors imagined a future Lagos after sea level rise and connected this vision to storytelling. This made me realize that geographic changes to a city affect many other aspects of urban life. The article also describes their playtesting, which showed that some information is difficult to communicate to players without background knowledge.
  • Transformation: players can imagine how a city might change and develop empathy for people facing this crisis.
  • Design implication: for a transformational game, narrative expression can help players deeply experience the theme and spark reflection. I want to use strong narrative elements so players become immersed. (Note: Climate Central and QuantumGIS can be used to create flood maps.)

5. The Mayor of Saint-Louis, Senegal

Films for the Humanities & Sciences, & TVE International. (2011). My City, Your City: A Senegalese Mayor Fights Sea Level Rise [Video recording]. Infobase.

  • What I learned: coastal cities like Saint-Louis, which contribute very little to climate change, are suffering some of its worst consequences. The climate crisis is not only an environmental issue but also a matter of international fairness and responsibility. In the video, residents block floodwaters with garbage, which creates serious health problems. This made me realize that disaster response also requires raising public awareness and that governments must actively intervene; but small cities like Saint-Louis find it extremely difficult to manage such crises alone and need international assistance.
  • Transformation: players develop more empathy for vulnerable coastal populations facing displacement, and recognize that many small cities need international support and that climate change is unfair to certain countries. The challenge: some players may question why they should care about a distant city instead of focusing only on their own nation's interests.
  • Design implication: let players take on different roles and perspectives, so they experience this unfairness from multiple sides and build stronger empathy.

Team Research Report

The three of us (Yunhao, Yihong and Zichen) wrote a research report together. Key points:

  • High-Level Purpose: Mobilize Urban Action on Rising Seas.
  • Why make a game about this: sea level rise is one of the most visible and existential threats of climate change; coastal communities worldwide already face displacement and infrastructure collapse; raising public awareness is essential to addressing the crisis effectively.
  • Central transformation: learn basic city planning methods (allocate flood protection, relocate populations and buildings, design a floating city); believe that climate change and sea level rise are real and urgent threats; see themselves as problem-solvers rather than bystanders.

Public awarenessCritical for effective policy; attention spikes during disasters but fades over time; the 6 months after a disaster are the key window to influence policy and public opinion (Green, 2024)
